Wind chattered through the leaves of the Deep Wood as the dark shape darted through the shadows of the ancient canopy. Thin arrow shafts of sunlight penetrated the murk. Where the light touched the forest floor, strange blossoms clawed their way out of the loam, only to fall to dust as as the sunlight danced away on the whim of shuddering boughs. The effect was a never ending wave of color that pulsed through the Deep Wood, like the steady breath of a sleeping behemoth.  
The creature moved in secret, for his purpose was the sort of mischief that never came to a pleasant end. Fast as he was, the light would occasionally catch on a tuft of fur — red as flame — or the swish of a snowy tipped tail. He moved silently through the Deep Wood, his paws avoiding every dry twig and crunch of dead leaves. Mice and other small rodents skittered out of his path, but they need not have bothered. He was on an errand, and he could not afford any distractions.  
He came to the edge of the wood, and looked out on a sprawling homestead. Keen eyes spied a figure in the distance. Large canine ears twitched at the sound of an axe splitting wood. The salty tang of sweat filled his nose. There was another scent beneath this, though. A familiar scent that set the thin white scar stretched across his muzzle to burning. Reynard the Fox let out a low growl and moved to stalk the figure. When the wards woven into the forest activated it was a subtle thing. No crack of thunder or flash of light. A weight settled over Reynard, rooting him to the spot. Reynard tried to set his will against the spell, but it was like trying to catch water with a net. When he bade his legs, move the mental command slipped through his will like grains of sand and his mind began to wander after the first syllable.

Reynard shook his head in an attempt to clear out the confounding effects of the spell. He stood upright and began to pace the edge of the wood, breathing deeply to dispel his frustration.
 
“300 years,” Reynard spat. “300 years I’ve been stuck in this wretched forest dodging fairy hunts, eating vermin, and drinking nothing but river water like some kind of animal. Me! Reynard the Fox — Prince of Tricksters, reduced to such a sorry state.” Reynard threw himself to the ground with a sob. “I don’t even remember the taste of wine anymore!” He carried on in this very un-princely state for several minutes before hopping to his feet, all sign of his sorrow gone. He stood upright, head held high as he ran his paws over his fur, smoothing it like a set of fine clothes.
 
“But all of that is about to change, isn’t it?” he chuckled. “Yes, everything is about to change.” He looked out at the figure still chopping wood. “Before your meddling. Before your betrayal. And you know what’s best of all? You don’t even know!” a yipping giggle boiled out of his chest. “It’s all coming to an end — all your hard work and sacrifice was for nothing— and by the time you see it will be too late.”
 
Reynard reached into a leather pouch at a belt around his waist and pulled out a large pale seed. He whispered to it for a moment and then rotated his fist with a flourish. A silver penny flashed in the rays that penetrated the gloom of the deep wood.
 
“For luck,” he spat and he flipped the coin high into the air. A raven swooped down greedily and snatched the tiny bauble, carrying it away from the Deep Wood. Reynard watched the bird’s flight until even his keen eyes could no longer see its passing.
 
Without another word or look back toward the figure at the homestead, Reynard the Fox melted into the shadows of the Deep Wood, his errand complete.
 
Miles away the raven dropped onto the branch of the sycamore tree, hopping along its length to the nest pressed firmly into the cradle of the trunk. The raven could not believe his luck! When he went to the Living Forest in search of baubles for his nest, he did not expect to spy the Sly One carelessly discard such a beautiful shiny. The crow dropped his treasure into the nest so that he could admire its glimmering beauty. His black eyes blinked, confused. He pecked curiously at the dull white seed that sat at his feet. It cracked open and a sliver of green vine reached out toward him. He cawed in disgust and kicked the wretched thing out of the nest where it bounced down the length of the Sycamore to come to rest in the tall grass at the base of the tree. As the first summer storm darkened the sky, the seed burrowed beneath the soil like a clam into the sandy surf.
